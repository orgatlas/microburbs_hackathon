Fit — does this home fit you?

A personal fit score beside the listing, with the facts that move it. City of Sydney trial · Chrome extension.

The same home. Different priorities.

Fit is the README’s Chrome panel for people browsing realestate.com.au and Domain. It brings Microburbs facts into a 0–100 fit score, four dimensions and up to three positives (“halos”) and three negatives (“failos”), each with its number and source. A six-question quiz sets the weights behind the needle.

Your priorities

Commute, Safety, Lifestyle and Price. The facts describe the home; the weights describe the person considering it.

Reasons you can check

A matched-address header and sourced facts make the score inspectable. Missing data is missing evidence, never a zero.

Scope: the product follows README.md; the scoring section documents the current side-panel code. Endpoint families in the README are candidate inputs, not evidence that every signal is live. Results remain pending below.

Sarah is not the median buyer

Sarah is 19, works casual retail for about $600 a week, and her parents are moving her out. She needs somewhere she can afford that gets her to work. The README’s default profile is rent mode, a $250 weekly budget and a workplace at Broadway, Ultimo NSW.

  • 35% Commute
  • 25% Safety
  • 15% Lifestyle
  • 25% Price

The clever step is quiz → weights

The six-question onboarding quiz turns answers into a stored profile and weights that sum to one. A work-focused person can put more emphasis on transport; someone who values nights out can give nightlife more influence. Sarah’s default puts the largest share on commute. It is a starting point, not a claim that all 19-year-olds share her priorities.

Personalisation changes the importance of the facts, not the facts themselves. It can also change a calculation: rental affordability uses the person’s income, and purchase affordability uses their budget. Parking and nightlife can respond to profile flags.

A useful tension: $250 is about 41.7% of Sarah’s $600 income. Her stated budget therefore falls into the README’s lowest rental-affordability band. “Within my budget” and “a low share of my income” are different questions. This is arithmetic from the persona, not a measured property result.

How the scoring model works

Fit is a weighted summary of available facts, not a valuation or a validated prediction. The panel recalculates its own score; backend scores do not enter the arithmetic.

  1. Read supplied facts
  2. Apply threshold bands
  3. Average each dimension
  4. Apply personal weights

Each dimension is the rounded average of its scored signals. Each signal counts once, except parking and nightlife: their profile flag gives full weight when true, half when absent, and zero when false. Missing or unreadable signals are excluded.

Fit is the rounded sum of dimension score × profile weight, divided by the sum of weights for scored dimensions. Empty dimensions are excluded and the remaining weights expand proportionally. No positively weighted evidence means “Not scored”, not zero. For example, only Commute 100 and Price 0 with Sarah’s weights gives round(35 ÷ 60 × 100) = 58.

Sarah’s default is Commute 35%, Safety 25%, Lifestyle 15%, Price 25%. Valid weights are scaled to total 100%; unusable weights in an otherwise recognised profile become 25% each. Missing or unrecognisable profiles use Sarah.

The three-pillar quiz conversion fixes Commute at 25% and shares the remaining 75% between Safety, Lifestyle and Price in the quiz’s proportions; it selects buy mode and maps the purchase budget. Car selection controls parking; buzzing / quiet controls nightlife. The four displayed tracks are read-only meters. Editing the saved profile through onboarding triggers recalculation.

Bands below include their written endpoints. Only transport, work and CBD distances taper: after the 50-point plateau, score falls in a straight line to zero at 1,200 m, 10 km and 8 km respectively, rounded to the nearest integer. All other rows use steps.

Source endpoints

The panel makes no Microburbs calls itself. Sources come from each returned factor’s source field (or “backend” if omitted). B means that supplied endpoint, with no fixed route assigned in the side-panel code. P is the parking fallback; T/A/C/H/V are endpoint examples in the adapter test fixture, and R is its rent test input—not proof of live coverage. Property paths use /v1/properties/{gnaf_id}/; suburb paths use /v1/suburbs/{suburb_name}/.

  • P: Property basics/all; property.parking_count takes precedence.
  • T: Property transport/nearby.
  • A: Property amenities/nearby.
  • C: Suburb crime/summary; the benchmark can be national, not necessarily the suburb.
  • H: Property risks/all.
  • R: Property rent-history-latest.
  • V: Property valuation/avm.
  • None: Social housing is explicitly rejected by the adapter and never enters the panel score; low/mid/high are reserved scorer bands.

The reasons beneath the needle

A signal’s share is its dimension’s effective weight × its share of that dimension’s signal weights. Halos (score ≥70) rank by score × share; failos (score ≤30) by (100 − score) × share. Keep at most three of each with positive contribution. Ties prefer the higher halo score or lower failo score, then signal order. Overall bands are Failo 0–30, Meh 31–69, Halo 70–100. Missing source dates are labelled missing; a fetch date is not the data’s as-of date.

How incoming facts are interpreted

The adapter uses 80 m per walking minute. Daily needs count categories within 800 m, not venues; unreported categories remain unknown, and the signal is scored only if those unknowns cannot change its band. Nightlife accepts a count, or one nearest venue within 800 m / ten minutes as one; multiple explicit counts are added.

Road-distance bands and DA-count bands apply when parsing distance or count text. Structured development numbers are already levels 0/1/2, not DA counts. Vacancy-percentage text and days-on-market text use the table’s bands; other structured negotiating numbers are levels. Livability and running-cost percentage comparisons treat ±5% as “at”; direct coded levels use their sign.

Nearest distance wins when several factors map to one distance signal; worst road/hazard level wins. Otherwise the first readable factor is used, except daily-needs aggregation and nightlife counts. Hazard flags, coded levels and explicit wording can establish “on property”; proximity alone cannot.

Rent must be positive and weekly income positive; buy mode needs positive AVM and budget. Weekly rent is converted from monthly, quarterly or yearly amounts when labelled. The other mode’s price fact is excluded. budgetWeekly does not affect rental affordability. The adapter can use the midpoint of a textual AVM range.

The panel consumes supplied work/CBD distances and the supplied comparable-sales percentage: it does not geocode work, calculate haversine distance, select three comparables or independently check data freshness. Counts are rounded and clamped non-negative; level codes are rounded/clamped to 0–2, except signed livability/cost levels.

Implementation reference: SCORING.md, score.js, adapter.js and profile.js. These rules describe the calculation, not measured matching accuracy or complete data coverage.

What data supports the panel?

Microburbs facts, keyed to a home

The README names the API at https://api-ht.microburbs.com.au. Address lookup uses GET /v1/geocode/address. Property facts use a gnaf_id; suburb facts use the API’s suburb name.

Named inputs include /v1/properties/{id}/amenities/nearest-transport, /v1/properties/{id}/basics/all, /v1/properties/{id}/rent-history-latest and /v1/properties/{id}/valuation/avm. The README also identifies comparable sales, crime benchmarks, risk overlays, nearby places, running costs, development activity and vacancy as starting-point endpoint families.

Exact available routes and fields must be checked in the Microburbs API specification. Naming an endpoint does not claim a successful live response or complete coverage.

Only the address comes from the listing

The README’s extraction order is structured address data (JSON-LD), then the page heading, then a user-typed address. No screenshots, transcription, listing description, photos or asking price are used.

The extension calls a local proxy. The API key stays in its gitignored environment file, never in the browser. The README specifies caching by address ID and suburb, and backing off when the API returns HTTP 429.

The README proposes a building-level social-housing estimate from the supplied flags file; the current adapter has no source wired for it, so it is excluded from scoring. Supplied CSVs and raw API output stay out of the public repo.

How we associate facts with the right address

  1. Extract or ask for the address. Keep unit and street-number information needed to distinguish homes in the same building.
  2. Geocode and filter coverage. The geocoder can return up to ten candidates nationwide. Keep only candidates within the City of Sydney’s 29 trial suburbs before trusting the top hit.
  3. Expose uncertainty. The README contract labels confidence as exact, building or guess, and includes candidates. The panel shows the matched address with a “Not this one?” picker and a typed-address correction path.
  4. Fetch by identity. Use the selected gnaf_id for property facts. A building-only match is not an exact-unit match; block or suburb data must retain that geographic meaning.
  5. Keep evidence attached. The README signal shape includes value, unit, label, endpoint source, as-of date and availability. Check the fact against the matched home, not just the original query text.

“105/8”, “Unit 105, 8”, “Level 3” and “8–10” are named matcher requirements in the README. The plan additionally proposes an index of the supplied address list before geocoding; that proposal is not treated here as a verified implementation. Success on difficult formats belongs in the held-out check.

City of Sydney only

The trial covers the local government area’s 29 suburbs, not metropolitan Sydney or all of NSW. The README specifies a calm outside-coverage state for an upstream HTTP 403. A nationwide geocoder result does not imply property-data coverage.

Missing, estimated and approximate

HTTP 200 can still mean data: null and available: false. That is “no figure”. Distance to work uses haversine (straight-line) distance, not a routed journey time. The CBD reference is Town Hall at −33.8731, 151.2069. Nearby hazards are distinct from overlays marked on_property.

Commute times shown in the panel are labelled “est.” and estimated from straight-line distance at typical mode speeds (walk 5, cycle 15, train/light rail 25, drive 20 km/h). The production version will replace them with real journey times from the Transport for NSW Open Data Trip Planner API, which Microburbs does not provide.
